Marco Alberto
De Benedetto

I am Associate Professor of Public Economics at the Department of Economics, Statistics and Finance, University of Calabria.

My research lies at the intersection of political economy, public economics, the economics of education, and labor economics. I use microeconometric methods and quasi-experimental research designs to evaluate public policies and study how political and institutional arrangements affect individual behavior, educational achievement, labor market outcomes, and the allocation and efficiency of public resources.

I hold the Italian National Scientific Qualification for Full Professor in both Public Economics and Economic Policy.

Working Papers

When the Right to Work from Home Works Submitted

with M. De Paola and M. Francesconi

Read abstract

We estimate the labor market effects of Italy’s statutory priority to work from home (WFH) for parents of children under age twelve. Using population-wide administrative data and a sharp regression discontinuity design, we find that eligibility raises mothers’ annual earnings by 1.6–2.3 percent in high-WFH-intensity firms, alongside increases in paid days and weeks. Mothers in low-WFH-intensity firms and fathers in general show no detectable effects. The estimates have an intention-to-treat interpretation and persist beyond the pandemic. Placebo tests show no effects before the pandemic, in firms that never adopted WFH, or among manual workers, consistent with a WFH mechanism.

Running a Tight Ship: Female Political Representation and Discretion in Public Procurement Submitted

with P. Giacobbe and A. Mosca

Read abstract

This paper examines whether female political representation affects the use of discretion in local public procurement. Combining Italian municipal political records with contract-level procurement data for 2010–2019, we exploit Law 56/2014, which mandated gender balance in executive committees above 3,000 inhabitants. We instrument the female share of aldermen with reform exposure, jointly determined by population eligibility and first post-reform election timing. A one-standard-deviation increase in women’s representation lowers direct awards and threshold bunching by 0.19 and 0.33 standard deviations, respectively. It also increases the number of bidders, without affecting repeat winners or worsening contract governance. Direct-award effects extend across several sectors, while disability-related procurement shows both a larger contract share and less threshold bunching. The findings are not explained by educational selection or mayoral gender, are larger in the South, and remain robust across alternative samples and specifications, placebo tests, and staggered difference-in-differences estimates.
